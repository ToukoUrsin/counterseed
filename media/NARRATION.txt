A thousand examples can suggest a rule. One counterexample can change it. This is Counterseed: a graph laboratory for finding the beautiful exception. Instead of asking a chatbot whether a conjecture is true, we write a precise question, search a complete finite universe, and keep evidence that anyone can replay. Everything runs in the browser, without an account or an API key.

Here is a tempting conjecture: if a graph has no triangles, then it must be bipartite. Counterseed checks graphs in a declared order: first by vertices, then by edges. It finds the smallest exception after five hundred and seven graphs: a five-cycle. There are no triangles, but an odd cycle cannot be colored with just two colors. The premise is true. The conclusion is false. One tiny graph breaks the universal claim.

The witness is not a picture pasted into a demo. Remove one edge and the actual graph changes. So do its chromatic number, girth, degree sequence, and both sides of the implication. The cycle becomes a path, which is bipartite. Restore the witness, drag the layout, or edit any vertex pair with the accessible adjacency controls. Twenty-four exact properties let us inspect the structure behind the answer.

Now we can ask something different. Does a connected graph with at least two neighbors at every vertex have no bridges? The answer is no. Counterseed finds two triangles joined by a single fragile connection. Remove that bridge and the graph splits apart. This is a custom logical expression, not a hard-coded result. The safe property language supports AND, OR, NOT, parentheses, and numerical comparisons, so students can build their own experiments.

Some conjectures survive. For connected graphs with even degrees, the Eulerian test constructs a walk that uses every edge exactly once and returns to its starting point. Here we check all thirty-three thousand, eight hundred and sixty-seven labeled graphs through six vertices. No counterexample appears. The interface says bounded evidence, not a theorem. Checking every small graph does not prove a statement about all graphs.

Every experiment leaves a paper trail. Field notes preserve the question and the finite universe. The downloadable record includes the search order, counts, smallest witness, exact properties, and a reproducible fingerprint. A separate command-line verifier uses independently written graph algorithms to replay it. Core properties were checked against independent oracles across the entire six-vertex universe. Counterseed is an original Open Invention project, built with disclosed AI assistance. Good questions grow from small exceptions.
